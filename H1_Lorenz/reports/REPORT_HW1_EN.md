## 1. Introduction

The Lorenz system, introduced by E. N. Lorenz (1963) as a simplified model of
atmospheric convection, is the three-dimensional dynamical system

$$\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}), \qquad
\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma(y-x) \\ rx - y - xz \\ xy - bz \end{bmatrix}$$

with the classical parameters $\sigma = 10$ (Prandtl number), $b = 8/3$
(geometry), and the Rayleigh number $r$ varied to select the dynamical regime.

The system is integrated with the explicit (forward) Euler scheme

$$\mathbf{x}^{n+1} = \mathbf{x}^{n} + \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^{n})\,\Delta t,$$

with $\Delta t = 0.01$ on $t \in [0, 1000]$ (100,000 steps). This step is well
inside the linear stability bound of explicit Euler for these parameters
($\Delta t \cdot \max|\lambda| \approx 0.6 < 2$), and the integration remains
bounded and stable for all runs below.

All computations and figures were produced by the accompanying script
`lorenz_hw1.py` (NumPy/Matplotlib); every figure in this report can be
regenerated by running it.

## 2. Part 1 — Periodic regime: $(\sigma, r, b) = (10, 15, 8/3)$, IC $= (0,1,0)$

### Theory

Besides the origin, the Lorenz system has the two symmetric fixed points

$$C^{\pm} = \big(\pm\sqrt{b(r-1)},\; \pm\sqrt{b(r-1)},\; r-1\big).$$

For $r = 15 < r_H \approx 24.74$ (the Hopf-bifurcation threshold), $C^{\pm}$
are **stable foci**: every trajectory spirals into one of them with a damped
oscillation. Numerically this appears as a transient "periodic-looking" motion
that settles to a constant — the center of the solution is the fixed point.

### Result

| Quantity | $x$ | $y$ | $z$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Analytical $C^{-}$ | −6.110101 | −6.110101 | 14.000000 |
| Numerical (mean of last 10% of run) | −6.110101 | −6.110101 | 14.000000 |
| |Difference| | 0 | 0 | 0 |

Starting from $(0,1,0)$ the trajectory is captured by the **$C^{-}$** lobe
(which of the two symmetric attractors is selected depends on the initial
condition; the other IC choice can select $C^{+}$). The numerical center
matches the analytical fixed point to machine precision.

## 3. Part 2 — Chaotic regime: $(\sigma, r, b) = (10, 50, 8/3)$, IC $= (0,1,0)$

### Is there an attractor? — Discussion

**Yes — but not a point attractor.** For $r = 50$ the fixed points $C^{\pm}$
are unstable, so no trajectory can settle on them. Instead, the motion remains
**bounded for all time** while never repeating: it wanders forever on a
*strange (chaotic) attractor* — the famous Lorenz "butterfly". An attractor is
a set that nearby trajectories approach and then never leave; it need not be a
single point. Here the attracting set is a fractal, non-periodic object:

| Variable | min | max | mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| $x$ | −25.61 | 34.08 | −0.03 |
| $y$ | −55.67 | 54.49 | −0.03 |
| $z$ | 0.00 | 108.18 | 47.56 |

The trajectory stays inside this bounded region for the entire $t \in [0,1000]$
(no blow-up, no NaN), switching irregularly between the two lobes. Because the
signal never becomes periodic yet never escapes, this is the textbook
signature of deterministic chaos: order (a well-defined attracting set) coexisting
with unpredictability (no repeating orbit).

*Fig. 3 — $x(t), y(t), z(t)$ for $r=50$: irregular, non-repeating oscillations
— the time trace of chaos.*

## 4. Part 3 — Sensitivity to initial conditions: IC $= (0, 1.001, 0)$

Both regimes were rerun with the $y$-component perturbed by only $10^{-3}$
($1.001$ instead of $1.0$), and the Euclidean separation
$d(t) = \lVert \mathbf{X}_1(t) - \mathbf{X}_2(t) \rVert$ was tracked.

![Sensitivity](figures/fig5_p3_sensitivity.png)

*Fig. 5 — Top: $x(t)$ for both ICs ($t \in [0,60]$). Bottom: separation $d(t)$
on a log scale over the full run.*

| Regime | $d(0)$ | $d(10)$ | $d(100)$ | $d(1000)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $r = 15$ (periodic) | $10^{-3}$ | — | $3.8 \times 10^{-5}$ | $0$ (machine zero) |
| $r = 50$ (chaotic) | $10^{-3}$ | $2.35$ | $24.0$ | $48.5$ |

### Discussion

- **$r = 15$ (stable):** the two trajectories are indistinguishable on the plot;
  the initial $10^{-3}$ discrepancy *decays* exponentially and both runs land on
  the **same** fixed point $C^{-}$. The system forgets the perturbation —
  no butterfly effect.
- **$r = 50$ (chaotic):** the trajectories agree only for the first few time
  units, then diverge visibly. The separation grows *exponentially*,
  $d(t) \sim e^{\lambda t}$ with $\lambda \approx 0.83$ (fitted on
  $t \in [2,12]$), until it saturates at the attractor's own diameter
  ($\sim 48$): the two runs then wander independently on the *same* butterfly
  but along completely different paths.

This contrast is the operational definition of chaos: **sensitive dependence
on initial conditions**. The tiny $0.1\%$ change in $y(0)$ is irrelevant in the
periodic regime and decisive in the chaotic one — the butterfly effect,
demonstrated numerically.

## 5. Conclusion

- With $(\sigma, r, b) = (10, 15, 8/3)$ the Lorenz system possesses the stable
  fixed-point attractors $C^{\pm} = (\pm 6.1101, \pm 6.1101, 14)$; from
  $(0,1,0)$ the computed center is $C^{-} = (-6.1101, -6.1101, 14)$,
  in exact agreement with theory.
- With $r = 50$ the attractor is strange: bounded, non-periodic, and chaotic —
  the Lorenz butterfly.
- A $10^{-3}$ perturbation of the initial condition dies out for $r = 15$ but
  grows exponentially ($\lambda \approx 0.83$) for $r = 50$, confirming
  sensitive dependence on initial conditions only in the chaotic regime.
